你交易，有人在数钱

很多人进入市场，关心的是买什么、什么时候买、什么时候卖。很少有人认真算过另一笔账——手续费。

这篇文章不讲K线，不讲策略，只讲一个被大多数人忽视的问题：你的钱，有多少悄悄流进了别人的口袋？

一、看得见的费用与看不见的费用

交易成本分两种。

看得见的：印花税、佣金、交易所规费。这些会出现在你的对账单上，清清楚楚。

看不见的：买卖价差、市场冲击、滑点。你挂单买入的价格和实际成交价格之间的差，就是你额外付出的成本。资金量越大，这部分越明显。

大多数人只盯着佣金费率谈，觉得自己谈到了万分之二很便宜。但他们没意识到，隐性费用可能比显性费用还高。

二、手续费的复利反噬

我举一个简单的例子。

中国股票市场的印花税目前是卖出时收取万分之五（2023年8月从千分之一下调）。假设你每天卖出固定金额的股票，一年大约240个交易日：

240 × 0.05% = 12%

在千分之一的时代，这个数字是：

240 × 0.1% = 24%

24%是什么概念？这是中国法律认定的高利贷年利率上限。也就是说，在不算佣金和其他费用的情况下，每天交易一次的成本，比借高利贷还恐怖。几乎没有机构能长期稳定跑赢年化24%的收益率。

更可怕的是，手续费吃掉的不是利润，是本金。每一次交易，你的本金基数都在缩小。这不是线性消耗，是复利反噬。

三、费用结构决定了市场的生态

不同市场的费用结构，本质上决定了什么样的交易策略能在其中生存。

A股：卖出收印花税（万分之五）+ 双向佣金。这套费用结构天然抑制高频交易，市场生态偏向中长线持有。

期货：没有印花税，但开仓平仓都收交易所手续费，按手数或按金额计算。费率低，适合日内和短线策略。

这不是巧合。费用结构就是市场的"自然选择压力"。在A股做日内高频，就像在沙漠里种水稻——环境不支持。在期货市场做长线持有，又浪费了低费率的优势。

理解了这一点，你就明白为什么不同市场有不同的主流策略，而不是简单地把一个市场的策略搬到另一个市场。

四、收益的正则化：一个机器学习的类比

做量化交易的人大多也熟悉机器学习。我想用一个机器学习的概念来解释手续费的本质。

在机器学习中，模型训练有两个目标：拟合数据（最小化误差）和保持简单（正则化）。一味追求拟合，模型会过拟合——在训练集上表现完美，在真实数据上一塌糊涂。正则化的作用就是惩罚模型的复杂度，逼它找到真正的规律，而不是记住噪声。

交易完全一样。

你的损失函数可以写成：

Loss = -收益 + λ × 手续费

"收益最大化"就是拟合——你恨不得抓住市场的每一次波动。"手续费最小化"就是正则化——它逼你减少不必要的交易，只在高确信度的机会出手。

这两者之间存在矛盾，就像机器学习中的偏差-方差权衡：

机器学习交易
欠拟合（高偏差）交易太少，错过机会
过拟合（高方差）交易太频繁，手续费吞噬利润
最优模型交易频率与收益的平衡点

一个不做正则化的模型，在训练集上漂亮但没有实战价值。一个不考虑手续费的策略，在回测里亮眼但实盘必亏。

如果你的策略加上真实手续费后就不行了，那它本来就不该存在——就像一个只在训练集上准确的模型没有价值。手续费，就是市场的天然正则化器。

五、回测中最诚实的检验

做量化策略的人都知道回测。但很多回测有一个致命问题：不计手续费。

我的经验是：回测时加上真实费用，是最简单也最诚实的筛选器。能扛住费用冲击的策略，才有资格进入实盘。

在我的策略回测系统中，10个趋势跟踪策略在黄金期货指数上跑5年，表现最好的 TMA 策略累积收益142%，但它一年只交易约10次。而 Bollinger 策略交易更频繁，结果却是负收益。交易次数和收益之间不是正相关——加上手续费后，这个差距只会更大。

手续费不会出现在任何技术指标里，不会在K线上留下痕迹。但它是每一个交易者必须面对的数学现实。

在你追逐下一次交易机会之前，先算一笔账：这次交易的预期收益，能不能覆盖你付出的所有成本？

如果不能，最好的交易就是不交易。

而你付出的每一分手续费，都有人在稳稳地收。你交易，他们在数钱。你亏损，他们还在数钱。这个世界上唯一稳赚不赔的生意，不是交易——是收过路费。

You Trade. Someone Is Counting.

Most people entering the market focus on what to buy, when to buy, and when to sell. Few ever sit down and do the other math — the math on fees.

This article isn't about candlesticks or strategies. It's about one question most traders ignore: how much of your money is quietly flowing into someone else's pocket?

I. Visible Costs vs. Invisible Costs

Trading costs come in two varieties.

Visible costs: stamp duty, commissions, exchange fees. These show up on your statements, clear as day.

Invisible costs: bid-ask spreads, market impact, slippage. The difference between the price you quoted and the price you actually got — that's an extra cost you paid. The larger your position, the more pronounced this becomes.

Most people haggle over commission rates, proud of negotiating down to 0.02%. They don't realize that invisible costs may dwarf what they see on paper.

II. The Compound Effect of Fees

Let me give you a concrete example.

China's stock market currently charges a stamp duty of 0.05% on sells (reduced from 0.1% in August 2023). Suppose you sell a fixed amount every trading day. With roughly 240 trading days in a year:

240 × 0.05% = 12%

Under the old 0.1% rate, the math was:

240 × 0.1% = 24%

What does 24% mean? It's the legal ceiling for usury interest rates in China. In other words, the annual cost of daily trading — before commissions and other fees — exceeds what loan sharks charge. Almost no institution can consistently beat a 24% annualized return.

What's worse, fees don't eat into your profits — they eat into your principal. Each trade shrinks your base. This isn't linear decay. It's compound erosion.

III. Fee Structures Shape Market Ecology

The fee structure of a market fundamentally determines which trading strategies can survive in it.

China A-shares: stamp duty on sells (0.05%) plus two-way commissions. This structure naturally suppresses high-frequency trading and favors medium-to-long-term holding.

Futures: no stamp duty, but exchange fees on both opening and closing positions, calculated per lot or per value. The lower rates favor intraday and short-term strategies.

This is no accident. Fee structures are the market's natural selection pressure. Day-trading A-shares is like growing rice in a desert — the environment doesn't support it. Long-term holding in futures wastes the advantage of low per-trade costs.

Once you understand this, you realize why different markets have different dominant strategies — and why blindly transplanting a strategy from one market to another rarely works.

IV. Regularizing Your Returns: A Machine Learning Analogy

Anyone working in quantitative trading likely also knows machine learning. I want to use a concept from ML to explain the true nature of trading fees.

In machine learning, model training has two objectives: fitting the data (minimizing error) and staying simple (regularization). If you optimize purely for fit, the model overfits — it looks perfect on training data but falls apart in the real world. Regularization penalizes complexity, forcing the model to find genuine patterns instead of memorizing noise.

Trading works exactly the same way.

Your loss function can be written as:

Loss = -Returns + λ × Fees

"Maximizing returns" is fitting — you want to capture every market swing. "Minimizing fees" is regularization — it forces you to trade less, only acting on high-conviction opportunities.

These two goals are in tension, just like the bias-variance tradeoff in machine learning:

Machine LearningTrading
Underfitting (high bias)Trading too little, missing opportunities
Overfitting (high variance)Trading too often, fees devour profits
Optimal modelThe sweet spot between frequency and return

A model without regularization looks great on paper but has no real-world value. A strategy without fee consideration looks great in backtest but will bleed money live.

If your strategy falls apart after adding real-world fees, it should never have existed — just like a model that only works on training data has no value. Fees are the market's natural regularizer.

V. The Most Honest Backtest Filter

Anyone building quantitative strategies knows about backtesting. But many backtests share a fatal flaw: they ignore fees.

My rule: add real-world fees to your backtest. It's the simplest and most honest filter. A strategy that survives the drag of fees earns the right to go live.

In my own backtesting system, 10 trend-following strategies tested on a gold futures index over 5 years tell the same story. The best performer, TMA, returned 142% cumulatively — but it only traded about 10 times per year. Bollinger Bands traded more frequently and ended up negative. Trade count and return are not positively correlated — and after fees, the gap only widens.

Fees don't appear in any technical indicator. They leave no trace on price charts. But they are a mathematical reality every trader must face.

Before chasing your next trade, do the math: can the expected return of this trade cover all the costs you're paying?

If not, the best trade is no trade at all.

And every cent you pay in fees, someone is collecting — steadily, silently. You trade, they count. You lose, they still count. The only business in the world that never loses money isn't trading — it's charging tolls.
