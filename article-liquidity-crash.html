当所有人都在卖：股灾的底层原理

2026 年 11 月，所长林超发布了一期名为《【深度】一次性讲透股灾的底层原理》的视频。视频开头先划清了讨论范围：这里说的不是经济衰退、企业盈利恶化导致的基本面熊市，而是基本面没有明显变化、却突然出现的流动性股灾（liquidity-driven crash）。

这篇文章把视频里的核心逻辑整理出来：股灾为什么能在基本面没变的情况下发生？为什么牛市之后反而更容易出现股灾？以及普通投资者怎样避免成为那个被迫卖出的人。

一、市值：最后一笔成交价乘上全部筹码

视频用了一个很直观的小区比喻。一个小区有 1,000 套房，平时每套成交价约 1,000 万元，那么整个小区的“市值”就是 100 亿元。某天半夜，一位业主急需 500 万元现金，把房子以 500 万元卖给隔壁小区的人。成交之后，按最新成交价 500 万元重估，1,000 套房的总市值瞬间变成 50 亿元——整个小区一夜“蒸发”一半，可实际上只成交了一套房。

股票市场也是同一套定价机制。上市公司的市值，等于最新成交价乘以总股本。视频提到，标普 500 当时的总市值约 68 万亿美元，但每天真正换手的只是很小一部分；绝大多数股票像小区里那 999 套没有成交的房子，只是等待最后几笔交易为它们定价。

市值不是“公司值多少钱”，
而是“最后一个愿意成交的人出多少钱”。

所以从第一性原理看，股灾的起点可以很简单：当所有人都想卖，而没有人愿意买，价格就会失去支撑。

二、股灾的主角：被迫卖家

平时大家为什么愿意持有好股票？因为可以选择不卖。但市场里总有一些人，在特定时刻会被规则逼着卖。

视频把这种人叫“被迫卖家”。他们不是不看好公司，也不是突然改变了对基本面的判断，而是因为保证金、赎回或风控要求，必须在最糟糕的价格上卖出。

杠杆是最典型的制造机。假设你有 10 万元本金，又向券商借了 10 万元，一共 20 万元买入股票。券商会设定维持担保比例，假设最低 130%，那么股价下跌约 35%、账户资产降到 13 万元时，就可能触及平仓线。补不上保证金，系统就会替你卖掉。强制平仓不看基本面，只看规则。

被迫卖家卖出股票，不是因为股票变差了，而是因为他们没有“不卖”的权利。

三、杠杆如何把一次下跌变成雪崩

视频用两个市场说明杠杆的连锁反应。

韩国：杠杆牛市后的强制平仓

2026 年上半年，韩国 KOSPI 指数半年上涨约 101%，6 月 19 日盘中创下 3,185 点新高。赚钱效应之下，韩国年轻人大量加杠杆：两倍融资是入门，三倍杠杆 ETF 是标配，甚至有人用 20% 的保证金去做 5 倍短期杠杆。6 月底，韩国股市融资余额冲到 38.6 万亿韩元的历史纪录。

这种结构下，只要先出现一次意外下跌，5 倍杠杆账户先被强平，抛售会压低价格，接着触发 4 倍、3 倍、2 倍账户的平仓线。量化基金、对冲基金、趋势基金、做空基金和杠杆 ETF 又会顺着下跌趋势加入抛售，最终形成滚雪球。

2015 年 A 股：跌停板与停牌

2015 年，上证指数从 2014 年年中的 2,000 点出头涨到 5,178 点，一年左右涨了约 1.5 倍。融资融券加上场外配资，据估算最高可能超过 4 万亿元，接近当时 A 股流通市值的 8%。

下跌开始后，杠杆崩塌的故事又叠加了 A 股特有的跌停板：跌停的股票卖不掉，投资者只能卖没有跌停的股票，抛压进一步扩散。视频提到，当时有 1,400 多家公司申请停牌，上证指数从 6 月 12 日到 8 月 26 日累计下跌约 45%。

杠杆不会让下跌更“合理”，只会让下跌更快、更集中。

四、机器交易：现代股灾的六台发动机

视频认为，杠杆是过去几十年股灾的最大推手之一；而在 AI 和算法交易越来越普及的今天，机器交易正在成为更大的放大器。

视频引用摩根大通的统计：美股中真正研究公司基本面的主动交易，可能只占成交量的 10% 左右；被动指数基金、量化机构和高频做市商等程序化交易，拿走了超过 60% 的交易额。

在这个市场里，六类“没有情感的机器”会在下跌中互相强化：

1. 高频做市商

代表机构包括 Jane Street、Citadel Securities 等。平时它们是市场最殷勤的接盘侠，但只赚价差，没有义务替谁挡子弹。一旦风险升高，算法可以在几毫秒内撤掉大量买单，市场瞬间失去承接，少量的急卖就能把价格打穿。

2. 期权做市商与末日期权（0DTE）

视频提到，2026 年 7 月，末日期权已经占到标普 500 期权成交量的 66.2%。当市场下跌，投资者抢买看跌期权，做市商为了对冲风险会卖出股票或期货；在负 Gamma 等特定持仓下，越跌越要卖，形成“火上浇油”的循环。

3. 波动率目标基金

这类基金的目标是把组合波动率锁定在某个水平。视频给出的简化公式是：股票仓位 ≈ 目标波动率 ÷ 当前市场波动率。平时波动率 10%、目标也是 10%，仓位可以打满；一旦波动率跳到 20%，仓位就要砍到 50%。市场越跌、波动越大，基金越要减仓，减仓又进一步推高波动。

4. 趋势跟踪基金（CTA）

CTA 的信仰是追涨杀跌：上涨趋势里可以一直买，一旦跌破关键均线就反手卖出。视频提到英仕曼集团旗下的 AHL 是典型代表。趋势一旦反转，这类基金会和下跌趋势同向交易，放大抛压。

5. 量化对冲基金

文艺复兴、Two Sigma、德劭等量化基金用复杂模型捕捉微小定价偏差，再叠加杠杆。但问题在于，顶级模型读同样的研报、喂同样的数据，很容易买入同一批股票。当第一家触发风控线开始抛售，持仓高度重合的其他基金也会被砸到止损线。视频提到，2007 年 8 月华尔街就发生过这样的“量化熔断”。

6. 被动指数基金与 ETF

视频称，2026 年美国被动基金规模已接近 20 万亿美元，占全美基金市场 55% 以上。被动 ETF 遇到大规模赎回时，不会像主动基金经理那样挑股票，而是按权重把指数成分股一起卖；权重越大的龙头股，卖出金额越大。当最抗跌的龙头股也被砸，全面股灾就临近了。

机器不看财报，也不听故事；它们只看波动率、趋势、风控线和赎回。基本面没变，机器照样可以卖出。

五、为什么股灾总在牛市之后

既然机器交易这么危险，为什么股灾不是天天发生？视频用美国黄石公园的森林火灾来解释。

20 世纪大部分时间，美国林业部门对山火采取“见火就灭”的策略。听起来负责，但小山火本来能清理森林地面的枯枝落叶；长期不烧，枯枝越积越厚。1988 年，黄石公园积攒了几十年的“燃料”被一场山火点燃，最终烧掉公园约 36% 的面积，动用数万人也无法扑灭。

视频把这个现象称为“自组织临界”：一个长时间平静的系统，本身就在积累下一次动荡的能量。对应到股市，一轮长牛市不管基本面有没有恶化，都会积累杠杆、拥挤交易和脆弱的流动性。没人知道哪一道闪电会点燃它，所以没人能精确预测股灾时点。

基本面决定资产长期值多少钱，流动性决定你能不能活到那一天。

六、普通人唯一的优势：不卖的自由

视频最后把视角拉回普通人。机器有算力、速度和执行力，但在流动性股灾里，它们会被自己的规则锁死在抛售链条上；人类反而有一个机器没有的特权——不卖的自由。

但“不卖”不是无条件死扛。前提是：不借钱炒股、不碰多倍杠杆、不拿急用的钱入市、不轻易 all in。最好还留好现金蓄水池，盈利时懂得谨慎止盈，看错逻辑时敢于止损保本。

视频还提醒：不卖的前提是资产本身有基本面支撑。垃圾股拿再久也未必涨回来；即使是宽基指数，如果买在 1989 年日经高点，账面回本也可能要等 34 年。所以“不卖的自由”不是盲目乐观，而是一套事先准备好的生存纪律。

股灾的第一课，不是学会预测闪电，而是确保自己永远不会成为被迫卖家。

不加杠杆，留好蓄水池，不要轻易 all in。

在投资世界里，胆小者生存，惜命者善终。

When Everyone Is Selling: The Anatomy of a Liquidity Crash

In November 2026, 所长林超 published a video titled “【深度】一次性讲透股灾的底层原理” — a deep dive into the mechanics of a stock market crash. He begins by drawing a clear boundary: this is not a fundamental bear market caused by recession or deteriorating corporate earnings. It is a liquidity-driven crash — a collapse that can happen even when the fundamentals have not changed.

This article organizes the core logic of the video: how can a crash happen while fundamentals are intact? Why are crashes more likely after a bull market? And how can ordinary investors avoid becoming the forced seller?

I. Market Cap: The Last Trade Price Times Every Share

The video starts with a simple apartment-complex analogy. Suppose a complex has 1,000 apartments, each normally selling for about 10 million yuan. The “market cap” of the whole complex is 10 billion yuan. One night, an owner desperately needs 5 million yuan in cash and sells his apartment for 5 million to someone from the next complex. After that single transaction, the whole complex is revalued at the latest price: 1,000 apartments times 5 million — 5 billion yuan. Half the “market value” evaporates overnight, even though only one apartment changed hands.

The stock market works the same way. A listed company’s market cap equals the latest traded price multiplied by the total share count. The video notes that the S&P 500’s total market value was then about $68 trillion, while only a tiny fraction of shares actually changes hands each day. Most stocks are like the 999 apartments that never trade — they simply wait for the last few transactions to price them.

Market cap is not “what the company is worth”.
It is “what the last willing buyer paid”.

From first principles, then, a crash can start very simply: when everyone wants to sell and nobody wants to buy, prices lose their support.

II. The Main Character: The Forced Seller

Why do people normally hold good stocks? Because they can choose not to sell. But some market participants are forced to sell at the worst possible moment.

The video calls them “forced sellers”. They are not bearish on the company, and they have not suddenly changed their view on fundamentals. They sell because margin requirements, redemptions or risk controls leave them no choice.

Leverage is the most typical machine for creating them. Suppose you have 100,000 yuan and borrow another 100,000 from a broker to buy 200,000 yuan of stock. The broker sets a maintenance ratio — say 130%. If the stock falls about 35% and account assets drop to 130,000 yuan, the account can hit the liquidation line. If you cannot add collateral, the system sells for you. Forced liquidation does not care about fundamentals; it only follows rules.

Forced sellers do not sell because the stock became worse. They sell because they do not have the right to say “no”.

III. How Leverage Turns a Decline into an Avalanche

The video uses two markets to show the chain reaction.

Korea: Forced Liquidation After a Leveraged Bull Market

In the first half of 2026, the KOSPI rose about 101% in six months and hit a record intraday high of 3,185 on June 19. Young Korean investors piled into leverage: two times was the entry level, three-times leveraged ETFs were standard, and some used 20% margin to run five-times short-term leverage. By the end of June, margin balances in the Korean market had reached a record 38.6 trillion won.

With that structure, one unexpected decline is enough: five-times leveraged accounts are liquidated first, their selling pushes prices down, and then the four-, three- and two-times accounts hit their own liquidation lines. Quantitative funds, hedge funds, trend funds, short funds and leveraged ETFs join the selling with the trend, and the avalanche feeds itself.

A-Shares in 2015: Limit-Down Boards and Trading Suspensions

In 2015, the Shanghai Composite rose from just over 2,000 in mid-2014 to 5,178 — about 1.5 times in roughly a year. Margin financing plus off-market leverage was estimated at more than 4 trillion yuan at its peak, close to 8% of the A-share free float at the time.

When the decline began, China’s limit-down mechanism added another layer. A stock at limit-down could not be sold, so investors sold the stocks that were still liquid, spreading the selling pressure. The video notes that more than 1,400 companies applied for trading suspensions, and the Shanghai Composite fell about 45% from June 12 to August 26.

Leverage does not make a decline more “rational”. It only makes it faster and more concentrated.

IV. Machine Trading: Six Engines of a Modern Crash

The video argues that leverage was one of the biggest drivers of crashes in past decades. But as AI and algorithmic trading spread, machine trading is becoming an even bigger amplifier.

Citing JPMorgan, the video says that active fundamental trading — people who actually study companies — may now account for only about 10% of US stock-market volume. Passive index funds, quantitative institutions and high-frequency market makers account for more than 60%.

In this market, six kinds of “emotionless machines” reinforce one another on the way down:

1. High-Frequency Market Makers

Representative firms include Jane Street and Citadel Securities. In normal times they are the market’s most eager counterparties, but they earn spreads, not obligations. When risk rises, their algorithms can pull large numbers of bids within milliseconds. The market suddenly loses its floor, and even a small amount of urgent selling can push prices through.

2. Options Market Makers and 0DTE Options

The video says that in July 2026, zero-days-to-expiry options already accounted for 66.2% of S&P 500 options volume. When the market falls, investors rush to buy puts; options dealers hedge by selling stocks or futures. In certain positions — negative gamma, for example — they have to sell more as the market falls, adding fuel to the fire.

3. Volatility-Target Funds

These funds aim to keep their portfolio volatility at a fixed target. The video gives a simplified formula: equity position ≈ target volatility ÷ current market volatility. With volatility at 10% and a 10% target, the fund can be fully invested; when volatility jumps to 20%, the position is cut to 50%. The more the market falls and volatility rises, the more the fund must sell — and that selling pushes volatility even higher.

4. Trend-Following Funds (CTAs)

CTAs are built to chase trends: they keep buying in an uptrend and flip to selling when price breaks a key moving average. The video cites Man Group’s AHL as a classic example. Once a trend reverses, these funds trade with the decline and amplify the selling pressure.

5. Quantitative Hedge Funds

Firms such as Renaissance, Two Sigma and D.E. Shaw use complex models to capture tiny pricing inefficiencies, often with leverage. The problem is that the best models read the same research, consume the same data and end up owning the same stocks. When the first fund hits its risk limit and starts selling, the concentrated positions of other funds are marked down to their own stop-loss lines. The video mentions the “quant meltdown” that really happened on Wall Street in August 2007.

6. Passive Index Funds and ETFs

The video says US passive funds had reached nearly $20 trillion by 2026 — more than 55% of the entire US fund market. When passive ETFs face large redemptions, they do not pick stocks like an active manager. They sell the index constituents by weight; the larger the weight, the larger the selling — and that includes the market’s biggest, most defensive leaders. When even the leaders are sold, a full-blown crash is close.

Machines do not read earnings reports or listen to stories. They watch volatility, trends, risk limits and redemptions. Fundamentals can stay intact while machines sell.

V. Why Crashes Follow Bull Markets

If machine trading is so dangerous, why don’t crashes happen every day? The video turns to the forest fires of Yellowstone National Park.

For most of the 20th century, US forestry policy was to put out every wildfire as quickly as possible. It sounded responsible, but small fires had been clearing away dead branches and undergrowth; with every fire suppressed, the fuel kept accumulating. In 1988, decades of fuel in Yellowstone caught fire and burned about 36% of the park — a fire that tens of thousands of people could not put out.

The video calls this “self-organized criticality”: a system that stays calm for a long time is itself building the energy for the next upheaval. In the stock market, a long bull market — whether or not fundamentals are deteriorating — accumulates leverage, crowded trades and fragile liquidity. Nobody knows which lightning bolt will ignite it, which is why nobody can time the crash precisely.

Fundamentals decide what an asset is worth in the long run; liquidity decides whether you live long enough to see it.

VI. The Ordinary Investor’s One Advantage: The Freedom Not to Sell

The video ends by bringing the view back to ordinary investors. Machines have computing power, speed and discipline, but in a liquidity crash they are trapped by their own rules in the selling chain. Humans have a privilege no machine has — the freedom not to sell.

But “not selling” is not unconditional stubbornness. The preconditions are: no borrowed money, no multi-times leverage, no emergency funds in the market, and no all-in bets. Keep a cash buffer, take profits carefully when things go well, and be willing to cut losses when the logic of a position breaks.

The video also reminds us that not selling only works if the asset has fundamental support. A bad stock may never come back; even a broad index bought at the 1989 Nikkei high might take 34 years to break even in nominal terms. The freedom not to sell is not blind optimism — it is a survival discipline prepared in advance.

The first lesson of a crash is not how to predict lightning. It is to make sure you never become the forced seller.

No leverage, a cash buffer, and never all in.

In investing, the timid survive and the careful live long.
