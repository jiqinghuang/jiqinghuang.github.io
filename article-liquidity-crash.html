当所有人都在卖：股灾的底层原理

所长林超最近发了一期《【深度】一次性讲透股灾的底层原理》。我看完最大的感受是：很多人对股灾的理解，从一开始就偏了——总以为暴跌一定意味着基本面出了什么大事。但他讲的恰恰是另一种情况：基本面没怎么变，市场却像被人突然抽掉了地板。金融学里把这种暴跌叫流动性股灾（liquidity-driven crash）。

下面是我对这期内容的整理，也夹带一点自己的理解。核心其实就三个问题：为什么没有坏消息也能暴跌？为什么牛市之后反而更危险？普通人怎样才能不成为那个被迫卖出的人？

一、市值只是最后一笔成交价

先看一个很朴素的比喻。一个小区有 1,000 套房，平时每套成交价约 1,000 万元，整个小区的“市值”就是 100 亿元。某天半夜，一位业主急需 500 万元现金，把房子以 500 万元卖给了隔壁小区的人。成交之后，按最新成交价重估，1,000 套房的总市值瞬间变成 50 亿元——整个小区一夜蒸发一半，可实际只成交了一套房。

这个比喻最扎心的地方在于：市值从来不是“这些房子值多少钱”，而是“最后一个愿意成交的人出多少钱”。股票市场也是同一套机制。上市公司的市值，等于最新成交价乘以总股本。标普 500 当时的总市值约 68 万亿美元，但每天真正换手的只是很小一部分；绝大多数股票就像小区里那 999 套没有成交的房子，只是等待最后几笔交易为它们定价。

市值不是“公司值多少钱”，
而是“最后一个愿意成交的人出多少钱”。

所以股灾的第一性原理并不复杂：当所有人都想卖、却没有人愿意买，价格就会失去支撑。基本面再好，也挡不住一个没有买盘的市场。

二、股灾的主角，是那些“不得不卖”的人

平时我们为什么能拿住好股票？因为可以选择不卖。可市场里总有一些人，在某个时刻会被规则逼着卖。

这里有个很关键的词：被迫卖家。他们不是不看好公司，也不是突然改变了对基本面的判断，而是因为保证金、赎回或风控要求，必须在最糟糕的价格上卖出。

杠杆就是最常见的一台“被迫卖家制造机”。假设你有 10 万元本金，又向券商借了 10 万元，一共 20 万元买入股票。券商会设定维持担保比例，假设最低 130%，那么股价下跌约 35%、账户资产降到 13 万元时，就可能触及平仓线。补不上保证金，系统就会替你卖掉。强制平仓不看基本面，只看规则。

被迫卖家卖出股票，不是因为股票变差了，而是因为他们没有“不卖”的权利。

三、杠杆如何把一次下跌变成雪崩

韩国是一个很近的例子。2026 年上半年，KOSPI 指数半年上涨约 101%，6 月 19 日盘中冲到 3,185 点。赚钱效应之下，韩国年轻人纷纷加杠杆：两倍融资是入门，三倍杠杆 ETF 是标配，甚至有人用 20% 的保证金去做 5 倍短期杠杆。6 月底，韩国股市融资余额冲到 38.6 万亿韩元的历史纪录。

这种行情表面热闹，结构其实非常脆。只要先出现一次意外下跌，5 倍杠杆账户先被强平，抛售压低价格，接着轮到 4 倍、3 倍、2 倍账户。量化基金、对冲基金、趋势基金、做空基金和杠杆 ETF 又会顺着下跌趋势加入抛售，最后形成真正的雪崩。

2015 年 A 股：跌停板与停牌

A 股 2015 年则是另一种剧本。上证指数从 2014 年年中的 2,000 点出头涨到 5,178 点，一年左右涨了约 1.5 倍。融资融券加上场外配资，据估算最高可能超过 4 万亿元，接近当时 A 股流通市值的 8%。

下跌开始后，杠杆崩塌又叠加了 A 股特有的跌停板。跌停的股票卖不掉，投资者只能卖没有跌停的股票，抛压进一步扩散。数据显示，当时有 1,400 多家公司申请停牌，上证指数从 6 月 12 日到 8 月 26 日累计下跌约 45%。

杠杆不会让下跌更“合理”，只会让下跌更快、更集中。

四、机器交易：现代股灾的六台发动机

如果说杠杆是上一代股灾的主角，那这一代市场里，机器交易的分量已经大到不能忽视。摩根大通有个统计：美股里真正研究公司基本面的主动交易，可能只占成交量的 10% 左右；被动指数基金、量化机构和高频做市商等程序化交易，拿走了超过 60% 的交易额。

换句话说，今天在盘面上决定短期价格的，很多时候不是看财报的人，而是一群算公式的机器。我把其中最值得警惕的六类参与者，按它们在下跌链条里的角色重新排了一下：

1. 高频做市商

Jane Street、Citadel Securities 这些做市商，平时是市场最殷勤的接盘侠，但它们赚的是价差，没有义务替谁挡子弹。风险一上来，算法可以在几毫秒内撤掉大量买单，市场瞬间失去承接。这时候哪怕只有少数人急着卖，价格也会被砸穿。

2. 期权做市商与末日期权（0DTE）

第二类是期权做市商，以及这两年越来越疯的末日期权（0DTE）。2026 年 7 月，末日期权已经占到标普 500 期权成交量的 66.2%。市场一跌，投资者抢买看跌期权，做市商为了对冲就得卖出股票或期货；在负 Gamma 等特定持仓下，越跌越要卖，等于往火上浇油。

3. 波动率目标基金

第三类是波动率目标基金。它们的目标是把组合波动率锁在某个水平，简化公式是：股票仓位 ≈ 目标波动率 ÷ 当前市场波动率。平时波动率 10%、目标也是 10%，仓位可以打满；波动率跳到 20%，仓位就要砍到 50%。市场越跌、波动越大，基金越要减仓，减仓又进一步推高波动。

4. 趋势跟踪基金（CTA）

第四类是趋势跟踪基金（CTA）。这类基金的信仰就是追涨杀跌：上涨趋势里一直买，跌破关键均线就反手卖。英仕曼集团旗下的 AHL 是常被提起的代表。趋势一旦反转，它们会和下跌同向交易，把抛压放大。

5. 量化对冲基金

第五类是量化对冲基金。文艺复兴、Two Sigma、德劭这些名字，用复杂模型捕捉微小的定价偏差，再叠加杠杆。问题在于，顶级模型读同样的研报、喂同样的数据，很容易买入同一批股票。第一家触发风控线开始抛售，持仓高度重合的其他基金也会被砸到止损线。2007 年 8 月华尔街的“量化熔断”，就是这么发生的。

6. 被动指数基金与 ETF

最后是体量最大的被动指数基金和 ETF。2026 年美国被动基金规模已接近 20 万亿美元，占全美基金市场 55% 以上。被动 ETF 遇到大规模赎回，不会像主动基金经理那样挑股票，而是按权重把指数成分股一起卖；权重越大的龙头股，卖出金额越大。当最抗跌的龙头也被砸，全面股灾就临近了。

机器不看财报，也不听故事；它们只看波动率、趋势、风控线和赎回。基本面没变，机器照样可以卖出。

五、为什么股灾总在牛市之后

聊到这里，一个自然的问题就来了：既然机器交易这么危险，为什么股灾不是天天发生？

黄石公园的故事很能说明问题。20 世纪大部分时间，美国林业部门对山火采取“见火就灭”的策略。听起来很负责，但小山火本来能清理森林地面的枯枝落叶；长期不烧，枯枝越积越厚。1988 年，黄石公园积攒了几十年的“燃料”被一场山火点燃，最终烧掉公园约 36% 的面积，动用数万人也无法扑灭。

这就是所谓的“自组织临界”：一个长时间平静的系统，本身就在积累下一次动荡的能量。对应到股市，一轮长牛市不管基本面有没有恶化，都会积累杠杆、拥挤交易和脆弱的流动性。没人知道哪一道闪电会点燃它，所以也没人能精确预测股灾时点。

基本面决定资产长期值多少钱，流动性决定你能不能活到那一天。

六、普通人唯一的优势：不卖的自由

最后，我想说说这期内容里最打动我的一点。

机器有算力、速度和纪律，但在流动性股灾里，它们反而会被自己的规则锁死在抛售链条上。人有一个机器没有的优势：不卖的自由。

但这句话很容易被误读。“不卖”不是无条件死扛，前提是不借钱炒股、不碰多倍杠杆、不拿急用的钱入市、不轻易 all in。最好还留好现金蓄水池，盈利时懂得谨慎止盈，看错逻辑时敢于止损保本。

还有一个前提是资产本身得有基本面支撑。垃圾股拿再久也未必涨回来；即便是宽基指数，如果买在 1989 年日经高点，账面回本也可能要等 34 年。所以“不卖的自由”不是盲目乐观，而是一套事先准备好的生存纪律。

股灾的第一课，不是学会预测闪电，而是确保自己永远不会成为被迫卖家。

不加杠杆，留好蓄水池，不要轻易 all in。

在投资世界里，胆小者生存，惜命者善终。

When Everyone Is Selling: The Anatomy of a Liquidity Crash

Lin Chao recently published a video, “A Deep Dive into the Mechanics of a Stock Market Crash”. I watched it twice. What stayed with me is this: most people misunderstand a crash from the very beginning, assuming a collapse must mean something terrible has happened to fundamentals. His subject is the other kind — the one where fundamentals are more or less intact, yet the market suddenly loses the floor beneath it. In finance, this is called a liquidity-driven crash.

What follows is my summary of the video, with a few personal reactions mixed in. The core questions are simple: how can prices collapse without bad news? Why is the danger greater after a bull market? And how can an ordinary investor avoid becoming the forced seller?

I. Market Cap Is Just the Last Trade

Start with a very simple analogy. Suppose a complex has 1,000 apartments, each normally selling for about 10 million yuan. The “market cap” of the whole complex is 10 billion yuan. One night, an owner desperately needs 5 million yuan in cash and sells his apartment for 5 million to someone from the next complex. After that single transaction, the whole complex is revalued at the latest price: 1,000 apartments times 5 million — 5 billion yuan. Half the “market value” evaporates overnight, even though only one apartment changed hands.

What makes the analogy sting is this: market cap was never “what the apartments are worth”. It is “what the last willing buyer paid”. The stock market works the same way. A listed company’s market cap equals the latest traded price multiplied by the total share count. The S&P 500’s total market value was then about $68 trillion, while only a tiny fraction of shares actually changes hands each day. Most stocks are like the 999 apartments that never trade — they simply wait for the last few transactions to price them.

Market cap is not “what the company is worth”.
It is “what the last willing buyer paid”.

So the first principle of a crash is not complicated: when everyone wants to sell and nobody wants to buy, prices lose their support. No fundamental story can hold up a market that has no bids.

II. The Main Character Is the Seller Who Cannot Choose

Why can we normally hold good stocks? Because we can choose not to sell. But some people in the market do not have that choice.

The key phrase is forced seller. They are not bearish on the company, and they have not suddenly changed their view on fundamentals. They sell because margin requirements, redemptions or risk controls leave them no choice.

Leverage is the most common machine for manufacturing them. Suppose you have 100,000 yuan and borrow another 100,000 from a broker to buy 200,000 yuan of stock. The broker sets a maintenance ratio — say 130%. If the stock falls about 35% and account assets drop to 130,000 yuan, the account can hit the liquidation line. If you cannot add collateral, the system sells for you. Forced liquidation does not care about fundamentals; it only follows rules.

Forced sellers do not sell because the stock became worse. They sell because they do not have the right to say “no”.

III. How Leverage Turns a Decline into an Avalanche

Korea is a recent example. In the first half of 2026, the KOSPI rose about 101% in six months and hit a record intraday high of 3,185 on June 19. Young Korean investors piled into leverage: two times was the entry level, three-times leveraged ETFs were standard, and some used 20% margin to run five-times short-term leverage. By the end of June, margin balances in the Korean market had reached a record 38.6 trillion won.

That kind of market looks exciting, but its structure is fragile. One unexpected decline is enough: five-times leveraged accounts are liquidated first, their selling pushes prices down, and then the four-, three- and two-times accounts hit their own liquidation lines. Quantitative funds, hedge funds, trend funds, short funds and leveraged ETFs join the selling with the trend, and the avalanche feeds itself.

A-Shares in 2015: Limit-Down Boards and Trading Suspensions

A-shares in 2015 were a different script. The Shanghai Composite rose from just over 2,000 in mid-2014 to 5,178 — about 1.5 times in roughly a year. Margin financing plus off-market leverage was estimated at more than 4 trillion yuan at its peak, close to 8% of the A-share free float at the time.

When the decline began, China’s limit-down rule added another layer. A stock at limit-down could not be sold, so investors sold the stocks that were still liquid, spreading the selling pressure. More than 1,400 companies applied for trading suspensions, and the Shanghai Composite fell about 45% from June 12 to August 26.

Leverage does not make a decline more “rational”. It only makes it faster and more concentrated.

IV. Machine Trading: Six Engines of a Modern Crash

If leverage was the main character of the last generation of crashes, machine trading is too large to ignore in this one. A JPMorgan statistic suggests that active fundamental trading — people who actually study companies — may now account for only about 10% of US stock-market volume. Passive index funds, quantitative institutions and high-frequency market makers account for more than 60%.

In other words, short-term prices are increasingly set not by people reading earnings reports, but by machines reading formulas. I have rearranged the six participants he mentions by their role in the selling chain:

1. High-Frequency Market Makers

Jane Street and Citadel Securities are the market’s most eager counterparties in normal times, but they earn spreads, not obligations. When risk rises, their algorithms can pull large numbers of bids within milliseconds. The market suddenly loses its floor, and even a small amount of urgent selling can push prices through.

2. Options Market Makers and 0DTE Options

The second group is options market makers, and the zero-days-to-expiry options that have become so popular. In July 2026, 0DTE options already accounted for 66.2% of S&P 500 options volume. When the market falls, investors rush to buy puts; options dealers hedge by selling stocks or futures. In certain positions — negative gamma, for example — they have to sell more as the market falls, adding fuel to the fire.

3. Volatility-Target Funds

The third group is volatility-target funds. Their goal is to keep portfolio volatility at a fixed level, and the simplified formula is: equity position ≈ target volatility ÷ current market volatility. With volatility at 10% and a 10% target, the fund can be fully invested; when volatility jumps to 20%, the position is cut to 50%. The more the market falls and volatility rises, the more the fund must sell — and that selling pushes volatility even higher.

4. Trend-Following Funds (CTAs)

The fourth group is trend-following funds, or CTAs. Their creed is simple: chase the trend. They keep buying in an uptrend and flip to selling when price breaks a key moving average. Man Group’s AHL is a classic example. Once a trend reverses, these funds trade with the decline and amplify the selling pressure.

5. Quantitative Hedge Funds

The fifth group is quantitative hedge funds. Renaissance, Two Sigma and D.E. Shaw use complex models to capture tiny pricing inefficiencies, often with leverage. The problem is that the best models read the same research, consume the same data and end up owning the same stocks. When the first fund hits its risk limit and starts selling, the concentrated positions of other funds are marked down to their own stop-loss lines. That is exactly how the “quant meltdown” of August 2007 happened on Wall Street.

6. Passive Index Funds and ETFs

Last, and largest, are passive index funds and ETFs. By 2026, US passive funds had reached nearly $20 trillion — more than 55% of the entire US fund market. When passive ETFs face large redemptions, they do not pick stocks like an active manager. They sell the index constituents by weight; the larger the weight, the larger the selling — and that includes the market’s biggest, most defensive leaders. When even the leaders are sold, a full-blown crash is close.

Machines do not read earnings reports or listen to stories. They watch volatility, trends, risk limits and redemptions. Fundamentals can stay intact while machines sell.

V. Why Crashes Follow Bull Markets

At this point a natural question arises: if machine trading is so dangerous, why don’t crashes happen every day?

The story of Yellowstone National Park is a good answer. For most of the 20th century, US forestry policy was to put out every wildfire as quickly as possible. It sounded responsible, but small fires had been clearing away dead branches and undergrowth; with every fire suppressed, the fuel kept accumulating. In 1988, decades of fuel in Yellowstone caught fire and burned about 36% of the park — a fire that tens of thousands of people could not put out.

This is what people call “self-organized criticality”: a system that stays calm for a long time is itself building the energy for the next upheaval. In the stock market, a long bull market — whether or not fundamentals are deteriorating — accumulates leverage, crowded trades and fragile liquidity. Nobody knows which lightning bolt will ignite it, which is why nobody can time the crash precisely.

Fundamentals decide what an asset is worth in the long run; liquidity decides whether you live long enough to see it.

VI. The Ordinary Investor’s One Advantage: The Freedom Not to Sell

The closing section is the part that stayed with me most.

Machines have computing power, speed and discipline, but in a liquidity crash they are trapped by their own rules in the selling chain. Humans have a privilege no machine has — the freedom not to sell.

But that sentence is easy to misread. “Not selling” is not unconditional stubbornness. The preconditions are: no borrowed money, no multi-times leverage, no emergency funds in the market, and no all-in bets. Keep a cash buffer, take profits carefully when things go well, and be willing to cut losses when the logic of a position breaks.

There is one more precondition: the asset itself must have fundamental support. A bad stock may never come back; even a broad index bought at the 1989 Nikkei high might take 34 years to break even in nominal terms. The freedom not to sell is not blind optimism — it is a survival discipline prepared in advance.

The first lesson of a crash is not how to predict lightning. It is to make sure you never become the forced seller.

No leverage, a cash buffer, and never all in.

In investing, the timid survive and the careful live long.
